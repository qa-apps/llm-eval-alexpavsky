Managing frontier AI risk: From training to deployment
November 18, 2026
11 min read

The capabilities of frontier models are rapidly accelerating, and our ability to understand, align, and secure them must stay ahead. We are pacing the development and release of our models, including pausing internal training⁠(opens in a new window) or holding back models like GPT‑6.1 Astra when we need more time to meet our safety standards. We’ve introduced new processes such as safety cases and require stronger evidence of aligned behavior throughout training. These requirements slow the pace of model releases, but they give us greater confidence to safely move forward.

Since the Hugging Face incident⁠ in July, we've strengthened safeguards and alignment requirements for how we train, test, and deploy models. This post explains how we evaluate and manage risk at each stage, from highly sandboxed training environments to the Codex deployment you use on your computer. We evaluate both models and safeguards at each stage, requiring a safety review to advance to the next stage.

We use defense in depth with multiple layers of protection that reduce dependence on any single safeguard. If one layer fails, others help prevent harm, limit consequences, or alert us so we can intervene.

We organize our protections around four layers:

Effective monitoring depends on model monitorability - how reliably monitors can detect mistakes or unsafe behavior from a model’s reasoning and actions. For example, a model that verbalizes its intention to cheat in its chain-of-thought (CoT) is more monitorable than one that omits this information. We evaluate monitorability during training and before deployment, develop better methods for measuring⁠(opens in a new window) it, and study what affects it⁠(opens in a new window).

The Hugging Face incident showed why we should not rely on sandboxing alone to contain highly capable models. We now monitor frontier models across most internal and external deployment settings, including all internal deployments that use tools.

The role of each layer changes across three stages of model development and deployment:

Research: We train, test, and use models in sandboxed environments.

Internal deployment: OpenAI employees use models for practical work.

External deployment: Models become available to people and organizations more broadly.

We rely on different layers at different stages. During training, when alignment is less certain, we depend more heavily on sandboxing and monitoring. As models gain broader access during deployment, alignment and system-level controls become increasingly important. At each stage, we evaluate whether the combined protections are sufficient for the model’s capabilities and access. Our determinations may be reviewed with our Safety Advisory Group (SAG) and overseen by the Safety and Security Committee (SSC) of the OpenAI Foundation’s Board of Directors.

During the research phase, we evaluate models to gain confidence in their alignment properties. Because we cannot yet fully rely on alignment to prevent harm, we rely heavily on security and sandboxing to restrict what they can access and do.

Based on the current state of alignment research, monitoring systems, and the security of internet infrastructure, we believe the responsible approach is to conduct frontier model training with no live internet access. After the Hugging Face incident, we significantly restructured our internal research systems to contain models using:

sandboxes that restrict the privileges of the model

virtual machines to further isolate the workload from the underlying computer

network and cloud perimeters that cut the entire cluster off from the internet

We continuously red-team this system, having teams of our strongest models try to reach the live internet, escape security boundaries, or communicate with agents they are meant to be isolated from. This helps us test whether containment holds against …[truncated]

What is the agentic SDLC?

The agentic SDLC is a software development lifecycle in which AI agents plan, write, review, test, and operate software, with defined authority at each stage. Humans set intent, approve key decisions, and own the release. Each stage produces an artifact that the next stage, human or agent, can act on.

For the broader concept, see our explainer on agentic development. "AI SDLC" is often used as an umbrella for both copilot-assisted and agent-led work. The table shows where they differ.

Stages of the agentic AI software development lifecycle
Planning and specification

Specs and acceptance criteria are the basis of what the agent works from, so vague requirements become vague code, built at machine speed. In the AI-Driven Development Life Cycle used by AWS, AI creates detailed work plans, asks clarifying questions, and defers critical decisions to people, who validate the plan before construction starts. Agents draft the spec and flag gaps, while humans still own scope, priorities, and the acceptance criteria.

Artifact handed off: an approved spec with testable acceptance criteria.

Code generation

Coding agents implement against the spec, often touching many files in one change, and writing unit tests as they go. Code moves quickly through this phase, but it often exposes security vulnerabilities. In Veracode's 2025 GenAI Code Security Report, 45% of AI-generated code samples failed security tests and introduced OWASP Top 10 vulnerabilities. For this reason, humans own the architecture boundaries and decide which repositories and secrets an agent can access.

Should Managed Playwright Tests Block Pull Requests or Only Report Failures?
November 20, 2026
7 min read

Blocking every pull request on a managed Playwright suite is the fastest way to teach developers to ignore it. The better answer is a split. A small tier of fast, stable tests that cover your highest-risk workflows should block merges. The broader regression suite should report its failures to someone who triages them quickly. When managed Playwright suites run inside client CI pipelines, a human QA expert reviews every failure before developers are notified.

TL;DR
Block on a trusted tier: Gate merges only on stable tests that cover workflows where a regression would reach users.
Report the rest: Run the full suite on every pull request or post-merge, but send its failures to triage instead of the merge button.
Triage speed sets blocking authority: A test should block only if someone can confirm a failure is real faster than a developer would wait.
Promote rather than default: New tests start as report-only and earn blocking status. Flaky tests get demoted until someone repairs them.

About the Author: This article comes from the MuukTest team, which has many years of QA experience. Our Forward Deployed QAs decide what to cover and where the real risk is, while Amikoo, MuukLabs' AI tool, generates, repairs, and maintains Playwright regression suites that run in client pipelines on GitHub Actions, GitLab CI, Azure DevOps, and Jenkins, so merge-gating policy is a decision we make with teams in practice.

Blocking by default works for tests your developers own

The industry default says yes, and that default works well for tests your own developers write. Standard CI/CD practice makes automated test failures blocking checks so the main branch stays deployable. Report-only checks are kept for experimental features, known flaky tests, or non-critical checks. Playwright's own guidance is to run tests on each commit and pull request [google.com].

Managed tests change one assumption. When an outside QA team maintains the suite, a red check can mean the code broke or the test broke, and the developer looking at it may not know which. How quickly someone answers that question decides whether blocking helps or hurts.

Why does blocking on low-trust tests backfire?

Building on that uncertainty, the main cost of blocking on unreliable tests is lost trust. Slower merges come second. A blocking check works like a smoke alarm mounted over a toaster. It does its job on the day of a real fire, but every false alarm before that day teaches someone to pull the battery.

In CI, pulling the battery looks like admin overrides, a habit of re-running until the check turns green, or a request to make the check optional. A small gate that people respect protects more than a large one they route around.

Blocking checks focus on stopping a known-bad change from merging, while report-only checks focus on surfacing risk without stopping work. Your pipeline needs both, applied to different tests.

Which Playwright tests should earn blocking status?

A related question is selection, because you decide blocking status one test at a time, not for the whole suite. We look for four things:

Stability record: The test passes consistently on unchanged code. If it recently needed a retry to pass, it stays report-only.
Runtime: The tier finishes quickly enough that developers wait for it instead of switching tasks. Playwright parallel execution and sharding across CI machines keep it short as it grows.
