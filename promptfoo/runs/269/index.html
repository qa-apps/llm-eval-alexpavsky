Red-teaming in AI safety is a structured process where people deliberately try to make an AI system behave in harmful, unsafe, or unintended ways—before real users or adversaries do. The goal is to find weaknesses, then fix or mitigate them.

In practice, AI red-teaming can include:

- **Adversarial prompting** – testing whether the model can be tricked into producing disallowed content, such as hate speech, private data, or instructions for harmful activities.
- **Jailbreak attempts** – trying to bypass the model’s safety guidelines or system instructions.
- **Bias and fairness testing** – probing whether the model treats different groups unfairly or produces discriminatory outputs.
- **Privacy testing** – checking whether the model leaks training data, secrets, or personal information.
- **Robustness testing** – seeing how the model behaves under unusual inputs, typos, role-play scenarios, or conflicting instructions.
- **Multi-turn attacks** – testing whether a series of seemingly harmless messages can gradually lead the model into unsafe behavior.

Red-teaming is often done by internal safety teams, external experts, or crowdsourced testers. The results are used to improve model training, add safety filters, update usage policies, and create better evaluation benchmarks.

It is related to traditional cybersecurity red-teaming, but in AI safety the focus is broader: not just system security, but also harmful language, misinformation, manipulation, bias, and other model-level risks.
